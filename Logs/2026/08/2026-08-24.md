### Narrative

This prompt asked for something the earlier calculation rounds hadn't done
explicitly: state the energy assumptions embedded in every category of the
model, and — if feasible — produce two scenarios, one at today's
fossil-fuel-heavy energy level and one at minimal fossil-fuel use. It also
explicitly said "Use Fable if you can," a departure from every "Use Opus"
instruction in the last several turns, so did this one directly rather than
dispatching subagents — consistent with the pattern that this project's
"Use X" instructions name which model should actually do the reasoning, not
just orchestrate it.

Ran four direct web searches to ground the work: current US primary-energy
and electricity-generation fossil-fuel shares (EIA, 2025: ~82% of total
primary energy, ~58% of electricity generation specifically); labor-intensity
comparisons between renewable and fossil electricity generation (mixed,
contested literature — mature wind/solar need far fewer ongoing O&M workers
per TWh than fossil, but construction-phase labor is much higher, and a
high-renewables grid needs additional storage/balancing labor); and
organic-versus-conventional-agriculture labor-hour studies (7-34% more field
labor for organic, which forgoes the synthetic nitrogen fertilizer that is
itself a natural-gas-derived input) as the best available proxy for a
minimal-fossil-fuel food system, since no direct study of that specific
question exists.

The core judgment call was recognizing that "today's mix" is not an
incidental detail of the existing model but something baked into literally
every category's numbers, since they all come from BLS/USDA/NCES/CMS data
describing how the US economy actually operates right now. Went category by
category through the existing script and reasoned explicitly about
fossil-fuel sensitivity: food and electricity are genuinely energy/input-
sensitive (synthetic fertilizer, grid composition); clothing and shelter/
furniture/freight are modestly sensitive (materials sourcing, fiber choice)
without a clear labor mechanism; and — the more interesting finding — K-12
education, essential medical care, and the ENTIRE non-traditional side
(childcare, eldercare, disabled care, household tasks) are essentially
energy-source-agnostic, because they are bound by how many hours of human
attention a task requires, not by what fuel powers the building it happens
in.

Implemented this directly in `CI_Reports/work_hours_needed.py`: added a full
"ENERGY ASSUMPTIONS" docstring section with the per-category reasoning and
citations, a `MINIMAL_FOSSIL_MULT` dict of reasoned (not researched)
multipliers, and threaded an `energy_scenario` parameter through
`traditional_national_hours()` and `compute_all()` so both scenarios compute
cleanly through the existing architecture without duplicating any logic.
Added a new "ENERGY SCENARIOS" print block to `main()` showing the
FOSSIL_BASELINE-vs-MINIMAL_FOSSIL comparison at all three LOW/CENTRAL/HIGH
columns plus a category-by-category breakdown. Ran the script twice — once
mid-edit to sanity-check the arithmetic against a hand calculation, once at
the end for a clean full run (exit code 0) — before trusting the output.

The result is a genuinely interesting finding, not just a mechanical
exercise: the headline CENTRAL total moves from 32.2 to only 32.7 hours/week
(+1.6%) under the minimal-fossil-fuel scenario, even though individual
energy-intensive categories move by double digits (clothing +12%, electricity
+25%, food +15%). The reason is structural — three-quarters of the whole
model (all non-traditional work, plus the two largest traditional categories)
is human-attention-bound rather than energy-throughput-bound — which reframes
the model's original "essential vs. discretionary" energy question as really
a "goods/materials vs. care/services" question. This is a good, non-obvious
result for the eventual report.
